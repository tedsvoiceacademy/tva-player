Set-up

Microphone inputs on that device

Turn the microphone on to see how many inputs this device has.

Each input you tick is recorded to its own file, and one more file holds them all mixed together. Untick an input with nothing plugged into it, or it records silence for the whole lesson.

Windows will not let an app do this by itself — it opens Settings so you can confirm it once.

The same loops and notes as your computer

Your marked parts, named parts and notes live in one small file per song, in a folder inside OneDrive. The Windows app prints that folder's path under its own Set-up. Point the phone at the same folder and a part you mark on either one is there on the other.

How it looks

Each one changes the colours and the finish together — some are lit like hardware, some are flat like modern recording software. Your choice follows you to your other computer.

Help

Getting a song in

Drag it into the window from File Explorer, or press Open a song at the bottom left. Add a folder lists everything playable in a folder down the left side for good; nothing is copied or moved.

Play all songs listed below plays the list on the left, top to bottom. If you have typed in the search box it plays what the search left showing.

A playlist is that list kept under a name. Save as a playlist names it; Open a playlist… loads one back and starts playing it.

The four dials

Speed makes the song slower or faster without changing its pitch. Straight up is normal. All the way down is quarter speed, all the way up is double.

Pitch moves the song up or down in half steps without changing its speed.

Pan turns one side of the recording down. Straight up plays both sides.

Volume is how loud the song plays.

Double-click any dial to put it back to normal. You can also click a dial and use the arrow keys.

The three lights by the song name

Playing lights while the song is running. Looping lights while a loop is repeating. Recording lights red while the microphone is recording.

Looping a part

Drag across the wave to mark a loop, or use Set the loop start and Set the loop end, which take whatever time the song is at. You can also type the times, to the tenth of a second, and nudge them with − and +.

Save this loop by name keeps it with the song, so "chorus" is still there next time you open it. Clear the loop only wipes the two times — your saved loops are untouched.

Recording

Recording happens on the player itself, to the right of the playback buttons. Record new (red) records just your voice. Overdub (orange) starts the song and records you against it — overdub is the studio word for singing onto a track that is already there.

Press either one and it records; press the same button again to stop and keep the take. You do not have to turn the microphone on first — it opens by itself. The Mic button turns it on early so you can check your level before you start, and Keep last saves the last two minutes the microphone already heard, so a good run is never lost for want of pressing record.

While the microphone is on, Your voice appears in the display with a bar showing how loud you are. Sing your loudest and keep it out of the red.

Turning a microphone up inside the app

Under the bar is a gain switch: − and + move it a decibel at a time, and double-clicking the number puts it back to nothing. It is the app's own gain, on top of whatever your interface is set to, so it is there when the interface has already run out. Turning a microphone up turns the room up with it, so use the least that gets you out of the bottom of the bar.

Each microphone keeps its own setting, and the settings are remembered against that interface — plug it back in next week and the levels are where you left them.

More than one microphone

An interface with several microphone inputs hands all of them over at once, and the app takes up to eight. Set-up says how many Windows gave and lets you untick any input with nothing plugged into it.

One take then writes a file for every microphone, and one more holding them all mixed together — so you can send one singer their own track, or listen back to the room. They share a name and a time, so they sit together in the list. With several microphones on, ◀ and ▶ beside the gain switch choose which one it acts on, and the panel says which that is.

Two things stay on the first microphone: the tuner, which can only name one note at a time, and Keep last, which saves the mixed sound rather than every microphone separately.

Watching what is being recorded

While the microphone is on, the bottom of the waveform becomes a live picture of what is coming in — one lane per microphone, named, scrolling from the right. It turns red while a take is actually being recorded, so at a glance you can see both that sound is arriving and which microphone it is arriving on.

Takes are listed under Takes and kept as WAV files on this computer.

Playing with the screen off

The song keeps going when the screen goes off, and appears on the lock screen with a play and a pause you can use from there or from a pair of headphones. The first time you press play, Android asks whether the app may show a notification — say yes. That notification is what makes the phone leave the song alone; without it the song stops when the screen does.

The same loops and notes as your computer

Under Set-up, press Choose that folder and point it at the folder the Windows app prints under its own Set-up — it is inside OneDrive. Both machines then read and write the same small file for each song, so a part you mark on either one is on the other. A copy always stays on the phone as well, so a phone with no signal goes on working.

What each machine knows about itself stays put: its folders, its microphones and its skin. A Windows folder list is of no use to a phone.

Getting a take out of the app

Every take carries two save buttons. Save my voice saves that take on its own. Save with the song makes a single file of your take and the song together, which is the one to send to somebody who was not there.

Either one opens the usual Windows Save box, and you pick the format from the Save as type list at the bottom of it:

  • MP3 — small enough to email or put on a phone. A forty-five minute lesson comes out around forty megabytes.
  • WAV — the full-quality file, about six times the size. A take saved as a WAV on its own is an exact copy of the take, so it happens instantly.

A long lesson takes a little while to save as an MP3. The button counts up while it works, and the app stays usable throughout.

The wave

On a stereo song the left channel is drawn on top and the right channel underneath, so you can see which side a part is on. A mono song is drawn as one.

Notes and the click track

A note is pinned to the moment the song is at; click it later to jump there. The click track plays a metronome over the song, with a count-in first.

How it looks

There are ten skins under Set-up — your own brand colours, a light one for a bright room, a plain grey one, a high-contrast one for reading the console across the room, and a few others. Each changes the colours and the finish together, and your choice follows you to your other computer.

Where your things are kept

Loops, notes and playlists follow you between computers through OneDrive. Takes stay on the computer that made them. The exact folders are printed under Set-up.
